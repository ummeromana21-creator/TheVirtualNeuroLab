Parkinson's: what losing dopamine neurons does to the brain's wiring Exploratory

In Parkinson's disease, the neurons that make dopamine slowly die. Fruit flies have dopamine neurons too, and in fly models of Parkinson's they are lost as well. Here we trace, in a real fly brain's wiring, where the dopamine signal goes and what is cut off as these neurons die, one at a time.

What dopamine does there

Known biology, from experiments on real flies, which helps read the map above.

Dopamine teaches the learning centre

Most fly dopamine neurons send their signal to the mushroom body, the fly's centre for learning and memory. Each dopamine neuron covers one small compartment of it. There, dopamine changes how strongly Kenyon cells (which carry smells) talk to the output neurons (MBONs), which is how a fly learns that a smell means food or danger.

Aso et al. 2014, eLife; review: Cognigni et al. 2018, Curr Opin Neurobiol.

Reward and punishment clusters

The large PAM cluster mostly signals reward (such as sugar); the small PPL1 cluster mostly signals punishment. PPL2 reaches the lateral horn, which handles inborn smell responses.

Liu et al. 2012, Nature; Burke et al. 2012, Nature.

Fly models of Parkinson's lose these cells

Flies carrying human Parkinson's genes lose dopamine neurons as they age. Different models report losses in different clusters, including PPL1 and PAM.

Feany & Bender 2000, Nature; Whitworth et al. 2005, PNAS; Riemensperger et al. 2013, Cell Rep.

Read with care. This maps where the dopamine signal would be lost and how well each neuron is backed up. It does not simulate the disease itself (for example the alpha-synuclein protein), why the cells die, or what the fly then does. Dopamine mostly acts slowly, by changing connection strengths, which the lab's fast simulation does not capture yet. There is no real-fly measurement of this kind to check it against, so it is labelled exploratory. Details and the analysis plan, written before it was run, are in the lab notebook.
