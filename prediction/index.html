Prediction check

How closely did the lab's predicted anatomy resemble the real flies?

Before we had each fly's measured cell positions, the lab predicted where every neuron sits, using only how it is wired. Now the real positions are known, so the prediction can be graded honestly. These numbers are the lab's report card, good and bad.

How we came to these numbers

  1. What was predicted. For each neuron, the lab took the brain regions its synapses are in and placed a dot at the average centre of those regions (centres measured on a different fly, the FlyWire reference), plus a small random scatter. Nerve-cord regions had no measured centres, so they were laid out by hand.
  2. What it is compared with. The real cell-body positions recorded in each fly's own scan (Maxime: BANC v888 release; Hagrid: male CNS v1.0 release). Only neurons with both a prediction and a real cell body are compared: .
  3. Same ruler, same pose. Both are converted to micrometres. The prediction was made in another fly's coordinates, so we turn, move and resize it (one rotation, one shift, one size change, no bending) until it lines up as well as possible with the real brain. That gives the prediction its fairest chance. The nerve cord is not used for lining up; it is simply carried along with the brain. The hand-drawn cord pointed straight down, while the real cord runs backwards under the body, so after this step the predicted cord sits in the wrong place (that is why it scores 0%).
  4. Shape resemblance %. We cut space into tiny boxes, 20 micrometres on a side (about a quarter of a hair's width), and mark which boxes hold real cell bodies and which hold predicted dots. The resemblance is how much the two sets of boxes overlap: 100% means the same shape, 0% means no overlap at all (statisticians call this the Dice score). It is an overlap score, not "percent correct": the prediction is penalised both for missing real boxes and for spilling into empty ones, and even two random halves of the same real fly do not reach 100% in small regions.
  5. Neuron placement. For every neuron we measure the distance from its predicted dot to its real cell body.
  6. A "no knowledge" yardstick. We shuffle which dot belongs to which neuron and measure again. In the headline cards the shuffle is across the whole brain (like putting each neuron at a random spot in the brain); in the region table it is only within that region, which is a much harder test. If the prediction were no better than the shuffle, the two scores would match.
Read with care. The rule aimed at where a neuron is wired; the scans record where its cell body sits, in the brain's outer layer. Part of every distance is that built-in gap. The comparison is also only as good as the alignment in step 3, which cannot undo real differences in how the two tissues were prepared. For example, Maxime's scanned brain is about half as deep (front to back and top to bottom) as Hagrid's while being about as wide, most likely from how that tissue was prepared; a fit without bending cannot correct that.
