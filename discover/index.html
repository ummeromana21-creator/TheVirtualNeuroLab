Knockout Lab · Discovery Screen 1

Switch off a neuron. Watch the signal find its way, or not.

A real fly brain, wired neuron by neuron. Put sugar or water on its tongue and watch the electrical signal travel along real connections to MN9, the neuron that makes the fly stick out its tongue. Then switch off one neuron and compare side by side: can the fly still eat or drink? As far as we know, most of these neurons have never been tested in a living fly: these are the lab's predictions.

    The predictions

    What we infer, and why it matters

    What these results tell us

    1. The feeding circuit has backups. Of 286 neurons switched off one at a time, only 8 lowered the tongue signal by more than 20%. For most neurons the signal simply finds another way to the tongue. That kind of redundancy is how a brain stays working when single cells fail.
    2. Drinking has more bottlenecks than eating. 7 of the 8 "needed" neurons matter for water, only 2 for sugar. In this brain, the sugar signal travels on many parallel roads; the water signal leans on a few key cells.
    3. A few cells are true chokepoints. Switching off "Roundup" #2 nearly silences drinking (98% drop) and "Clavicle" cuts it by 84%. In the map you can see the route collapse.
    4. Three new candidates. CB0192, CB0051 and CB0501 are predicted to be needed for drinking, and we found no living-fly test of them. These are new, testable predictions.

    How this can help in the real world

    • Where to look first. Testing every neuron in a living fly takes years. A screen like this narrows 286 candidates down to 3 untested ones a lab could check next, saving time and animals.
    • A method that scales. The same switch-off-and-watch approach works for any behaviour with a known motor neuron, and for any other brain once it has been mapped.
    • Understanding thirst. Knowing which few cells carry the "drink" signal helps explain how brains control fluid intake, a basic survival system every animal shares in some form.
    What it doesn't tell us. These are predictions, not facts. When we checked this brain against known results it scored 10 of 15, and its misses were exactly this kind of single-neuron question. Each of the 8 is a strong lead that still needs a living-fly experiment.
