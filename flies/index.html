What's happening in the brain

    Brain regions that responded

    Round 1: an exam on things already known

    Why test results that are already known? These are not discoveries. Each one was found in living flies years ago. We use them as an exam for the simulated brain, the way you'd test a new thermometer in water you already know is 100 °C.
    • The brain was not adjusted to pass. The wiring comes straight from the electron-microscopy reconstruction and the settings are exactly the ones published in 2024. We didn't change a single number to make it match.
    • The pass mark was written down before the run, with a timestamp, so we couldn't move the goalposts once we saw the answer. The fingerprint on each card would change if anything were edited later.
    • Why it matters: the score tells you how far to trust the brain when it predicts things nobody has tested yet, which is what the Neuron Silencing Screen does. The misses matter too: they show where it's weaker.

    What we infer, and why it matters

    What these results tell us

    1. Much of taste behaviour is written in the wiring. With nothing but the scanned connections and one simple rule for how neurons fire, the brain gets the basics right: sugar brings the tongue out, more sugar pushes harder, bitter alone does nothing, and water works through its own neurons.
    2. Bitter is built to win. Here, bitter cuts the sugar response from 65 to 0.1 firings a second, and with strong bitter, even double-strength sugar barely gets through (11 a second). A "safety first" design, which makes sense: tasting poison should overrule wanting food.
    3. Signals add up. Sugar and water together push the tongue neuron harder than either alone (83 vs 65 for sugar).
    4. Wiring isn't the whole story. The brain passed 10 of 15 checks. Results driven by whole groups of sensory neurons came out right; the misses all involve single named neurons. Something beyond the wiring, such as chemical signals for hunger or thirst, seems to shape those.

    How this can help in the real world

    • Fewer animal experiments. A brain that passes known checks can be used to try ideas first, so real labs only test the most promising ones. The Neuron Silencing Screen does exactly this.
    • Trust, measured. Brain simulations are becoming a common research tool. Scoring one honestly against known results, misses included, shows when its predictions are worth acting on.
    • How brains weigh reward against danger. The bitter-beats-sugar rule is a simple, fully mapped example of a decision every animal makes. Flies are widely used to study how brains work because so much of their biology is shared with ours: most genes linked to human disease have a counterpart in the fly (Reiter et al. 2001, Genome Research).
    • Insect feeding. Knowing which taste signals stop an insect from eating is the basis of feeding deterrents. Whether this fly's wiring carries over to pest species would need testing in those species.
    What it doesn't tell us. This is a model of one female fly's brain, with no body, no hunger or thirst state and no chemical messengers. It shows what the wiring can do, not everything a living fly does.

    How to read this

    • Who chose the Round 1 tests? A rule written before any run: every result in the 2024 brain-model study (Shiu et al., Nature) that was tested in living flies, names its neurons, and uses neurons listed in the authors' public code. That rule includes results the original model got wrong, so we can't pick only the easy ones.
    • Can the lab bend itself to match? No. The brain uses the published settings and never sees the real-fly answer. The fingerprint on each card would change if the engine, the wiring or the saved rules were edited.
    • What a miss means: wiring plus this simple neuron model isn't enough to explain that behaviour. That's useful: it points to what the model is missing (for example, chemical signals that aren't in the wiring diagram).
    • Which brain? Everything here runs on the FlyWire brain, which passed the calibration test first. Every run is written up in the lab notebook.
